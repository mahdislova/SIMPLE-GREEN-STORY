Episode 1

“The Engagement”

49 Days to First Snow

We open in 1489. A gallows field at night. Something huge is breathing underground in a pit of iron, and all we see is one hand: blue ice, the size of a cartwheel, with an iron hook through it. A man named Alistair Harding cuts its wrist. The blood runs green and ignites inside a lantern, the first gallowglass light. His boy, nine years old, holds the lantern up for him, proud. Behind them a tiny girl made of frost slips out of the pit and runs into the dark. At the treeline, a young woman in Harding colours sees everything and backs away before anyone notices her.

Cut to 1889. London glows green. Every lamp, every tram and every furnace burns gallowglass, a crystal the Harding family grows at their Works on the old gallows field. That’s the official story.

A rain-slicked roofline in the Narrows. Two figures cross it without a sound.

The first is Alice Bounds, twenty-one today, though nobody has said so. She is small and coiled, five foot nothing of wire and grudge. Her dark hair is chopped short with a knife and pinned flat to her skull so nothing can grab it. Her knuckles are scabbed over old yellow bruises. She wears a short coat of oiled black wool, a red Vykra knot stitched inside the left glove where only the guild will ever see it, and soft boots wrapped in rag so the slates won’t talk. She moves like she’s expecting to be hit and has already decided where.

The second is her sister Miriam, twenty-three, half a head taller and softer in the face. Her chestnut hair is up in a careful coil she spent too long on, and her coat is buttoned to the throat. She’s the patient one, the one with the plan. She puts her hands flat on things, tables and walls and her sister’s shoulder, as if checking they’re still there.

“Twelve guards,” Alice says. “Four on the door, two on the stair.”

“And?”

“And nothing. I’m counting so you don’t have to.”

“You always count. You never wait.” Miriam checks the sky, then the street. “Fast. Quiet. Home before the bells.”

“You have somewhere to be.”

Miriam doesn’t answer. Alice notices. Alice always notices.

Below them, in a lamplit storeroom, crates that aren’t crates stand open, and a line of girls is roped together at the wrist. A trafficker called Kestrel is selling. The man buying wears a good grey coat and has a pleasant, forgettable face and cold eyes; he counts heads the way other men count coin, and he’s eating an apple. His name is Ajax Fink. “Then twelve reach the brothels,” he says, “and two don’t reach anywhere.” Above the grate, Miriam has stopped counting exits. “Miriam. We’re here for the ledger.” “I know what we’re here for.”

Then they drop. Six seconds a man. Thk. Thk-thk. Hhnk. Alice goes through the room like weather. Miriam walks to Kestrel’s table, puts her hand flat on it and says, “I know exactly what they are. That’s why I’m here.” It isn’t a fight. It’s punctuation. Ajax doesn’t run. He walks out the back with the ledger’s first pages under his arm and stops at the door to look straight at Miriam. He files her face and smiles. “Tell your patron the house is closed.”

A steam laundry in Shadwell, after midnight. Mother Grise, head of the Vykra, is a woman in her sixties with steel spectacles, iron-grey hair scraped into a bun, a black bombazine dress gone green at the seams, and a clerk’s hands, ink in the creases and nails filed square. She counts a purse onto the table, tik, tik, tik, heavier than the job. “The patron should know a man walked out with the first pages. Lambrick’s man.” The Vykra protect the Lanes’ children. That’s how they recruit. It isn’t why they were founded.

At the Harding Works, Nathaniel Harding, twenty-one, the second son, straps on a prototype jump-gauntlet he’s been building alone. He rockets across the foundry, FWOOMPH, and lands in a coal bin. Byron, the old butler, picks soot out of his hair. They’ve been doing this for years, and the butler is his only audience. Tonight Nathaniel plans to show his father.

At Harding House, the patriarch Eldgard tells his eldest son Jamie that tonight begins “his preparation.” Jamie says he already knows what the Tithe is. He leaves his pocket watch on his father’s desk, wound, like a man who won’t need to know the time any more.

At the Lantern Guard tower, Nixon Hargrave, twenty-three, becomes the youngest captain in the corps. Eldgard pins the glass lantern to his collar: “My family looks after its own.” A seventeen-year-old recruit named Cole tries to salute and smacks himself in the eye.

• • •

A rented room over a laundry, warm, cheap and theirs. The window sweats. Downstairs the coppers thump, and the whole floor smells of lye and wet cotton. Miriam is at a cracked mirror doing her hair, more carefully than she does anything else. Alice sprawls on the cot eating stolen bread.

“You’ve done your hair three times.”

“Twice.”

“Three.”

“Then stop watching.”

“I’m going to find out who he is.”

“You are not.”

“I’m going to follow you one night and ruin everything.”

Miriam smiles at the mirror. “You’d have to leave the house. You hate leaving the house.”

Alice laughs, really laughs, for the only time this episode. Then Miriam says, casually, without looking up: “I don’t need you to know. Not yet.”

“Why not?”

“Because it isn’t real until it’s mine.”

Alice’s smile goes strange. She looks at her hands. “Do you know what today’s date is?”

Miriam stops, pin in her teeth. “…Oh.”

“It’s fine.”

“Oh, shoot — I’m sorry. Happy birthday, dear sister.” She turns, stricken. “I’ll make it up to you. I will. Tomorrow—”

“Tomorrow.”

Alice looks away, and for one unguarded second we see it clearly: not anger but yearning. She wants what Miriam has, someone to get ready for.

Miriam takes her coat and pauses at the door. There’s something she decides not to say. “Lock the door.”

On the landing, alone, she stops at a mirror with the silvering gone, looks at herself for a moment, and puts a hand flat on her stomach.

Inside, Alice sits on the cot with the latch in her hand for a full minute. Click. Unclick. Then she takes her coat.

She tails her sister through the fog toward Mayfair, half a street behind, the way she was trained. She loses her where the carriages choke the street outside Harding House: two hundred horses, a thousand green windows, and Miriam’s careful coil gone into a crowd of footmen. On a hook by the service door hangs a lamp-girl’s apron. Alice puts it on. She goes in looking for whoever her sister did her hair for.

A ball at Harding House, under a thousand green chandeliers. Nathaniel’s engagement to Tamara Lambrick is announced; their fathers arranged it. Tamara is twenty-two and wears pale grey silk cut for a funeral, every button closed; her black hair is braided tight, her eyes don’t stop moving, and she carries a cipher wheel inside her fan. She and Nathaniel agree to be very polite to each other for forty years. Across the room, Nixon watches Tamara and looks away when she looks back.

Nathaniel steps into the light to show his father the gauntlet. Eldgard, smiling for the room: “Not tonight. Toys after supper.” Laughter. Nathaniel’s face crumbles. On his way out he passes Nixon at the door. “He pinned your lantern himself today. You’re the son he actually wanted, Nix.”

The lights go out.

Not just the ballroom: London. Street by street, the green dies across the city. In the black ballroom, Nathaniel hears a woman crying inside the walls. Nobody else does. A match flares, tssk: the lamp-girl, cupping it, the only light in the room. He finds her by it.

“Do you hear that?”

“Hear what, sir?”

The match burns down to her fingers and she doesn’t flinch. He notices. On her way out she lifts the schematic of his gauntlet from his coat, because she’s a Vykra blade and it was there. She came here to find a man. She found the wrong one.

Below the Works, Jamie goes down thirteen steps with a glass case under his arm: the little green “turtle” from his mother’s drawing room, a curio nobody ever looked at twice. He sets it at the foot of an iron lock and cuts at the iron with a crystal blade until his hand bleeds into it. The chain shudders. Alarms. Nixon leads his squad down into strobe-lit darkness, a masked figure at the lock, and fires. KRAK. The figure drops. The glass case shatters beside him, and something small and cold goes down the blood channels into the dark. Nixon pulls off the mask. It’s Jamie.

Eldgard steps out of the dark. “Go, Captain. Wait at the gate. Speak to no one.”

• • •

Upstairs, the lamps stutter back on. Edmund Lambrick arrives late. He is Lord Lambrick’s bastard son, twenty, raised in the house and preferred as heir over his sister: tall, fair, his hair oiled into a careful wave, handsome in a way that makes dinner guests laugh at whatever he says. His evening coat is midnight wool with one button missing. His collar is askew. His hands are damp, recently washed. He takes a glass of champagne from a passing tray and drinks it in one pull.

“You look terrible,” Tamara says.

“I’m fine,” he says, too quickly. “Just the cold.”

He walks away. Nobody notices.

Blackwood Bridge, in fog thick enough to chew. Miriam stands at the railing, dressed for an evening she never got to. She was poisoned hours ago and can barely stand. She is waiting for a man who isn’t coming, and only now understanding it. She came out tonight to tell one person good news. She never gets to.

She takes a letter from her pocket and reads it one last time. “I’m sorry, Alice.”

She climbs the railing. She doesn’t hesitate.

She hits the water where the buried Tyburn spills out under the arch, ploonsh, and the water boils green. Something that was just freed across the city pours into her eyes and her mouth.

SMASH CUT TO BLACK.

Episode 2

“The Wake”

47 Days to First Snow

Dawn. Eldgard tells the family Jamie died in an accident. Nixon finds Nathaniel broken in the stables and holds him, with his best friend’s brother’s blood still under his fingernails.

The central police ward: green lamps, wet stone, a drain in the floor that never stops talking. Inspector Felix Hawke, forty, rumpled, his coat still wet from the river, a pencil behind his ear and egg on his tie, is looking at a girl on a bench at the end of the ward. She was fished out under Blackwood Bridge alive, and nobody can say how. She sits wrapped in a police blanket with her bare feet off the floor, water still dripping from her hair, perfectly still.

Nathaniel is there to shout about his brother’s file. Halfway down the ward he stops. The crying he heard inside the walls of his house is coming from her. He goes grey and leaves. In the doorway he nearly bowls over a woman who has run the whole way from Gin Lane with her coat open. He doesn’t register her. She registers him: the man with the match.

Alice looks at the girl on the bench. “That’s my sister.” She takes her home.

The tenement room. Alice settles her on the cot and gets nothing back, not grief or anger, just the way someone doesn’t answer a language they don’t speak yet. A neighbour’s door slams on the landing, BANG. Alice flinches, as she always has. The girl on the cot doesn’t move. All their lives the real Miriam would have stepped between Alice and any sudden sound. Alice notices the gap before she understands it.

Jamie’s funeral at St Bride’s, the Harding church. Nixon carries the coffin and it’s wrong in his hands, heavier than a man and dead still. Nathaniel gives the eulogy and breaks halfway through. Among the Harding brasses on the wall: Ottoline Harding, 1820–1839, Died Abroad. Nobody explains who she was.

Nathaniel forces his way into the Works and finds a spent Lantern Guard rifle cartridge where his brother “fell.” He gives it to Nixon: “This is one of yours. Find me the one who was down there.” Nixon takes the case. He is now investigating himself.

Josephine Harding sits alone in a tea room with her wedding ring on the table and the pale band showing on her finger. She is fifty, straight-backed and silver at the temples, in mourning black so good it looks like money, with very still hands. Mother Grise sits down across from her. “The Harding boy. Your own son.” “He isn’t digging. He’s grieving. But grief makes people thorough. And thorough people find things.” “And if he finds something he shouldn’t?” “Then he’ll have found it too late.” She puts the ring back on.

A knock at the tenement. Folded paper, no crest. Shadow an aristocrat: Nathaniel Harding. Report what he finds. If he comes too close to something dangerous, redirect him. “Who’s the patron?” “Need to know.” Alice looks at the name, then at the closed door where her sister isn’t sleeping. She takes it.

• • •

A cellar clinic under Gin Lane: dripping pipes, a stove, tables built for amputations, a bucket nobody looks into. The street surgeon is a big, stooped man in a leather apron over a collarless shirt, with sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms scarred from the factory floor, and a face like a closed shop. He’s used to factory amputations, not miracles. He checks the girl’s pulse and eyes. Then he stops and looks at Alice over her head.

“When did she lose it?”

“Lose what?”

He wipes his hands and doesn’t answer at once, which is the worst thing he does all season. “She was carrying. Two months, perhaps three. The fall, or whatever she took before it, either would have done it. Both together, there was never a chance.”

“You’re wrong.”

“I’m not wrong. I’m sorry. I’m not wrong.”

Alice kneels and takes her sister’s face in both hands. “Who.”

“A nobleman.”

“Which one.”

The girl searches and finds only this: “From the party. At the Hardings’.”

Alice, barely audible: “You said it wasn’t real yet.”

She came to find out what was wrong with her sister. She leaves knowing Miriam had a whole life she was never told about, and chose to keep it from her. She also leaves with a reason to want the job she took that morning. It gets her into Harding rooms.

That night Alice breaks into Jamie’s bedroom and finds Nathaniel already there. They end up hiding chest to chest in Jamie’s wardrobe among his coats, which smell of cedar and cigar, while Eldgard comes in alone with a candle, sits on his dead son’s bed and winds Jamie’s pocket watch. Tk-tk-tk-tk. He doesn’t say a word. Nathaniel nearly steps out. Alice puts her hand over his mouth. Among Jamie’s things they find a diagram of the chain under the Works, and below it a second staircase of thirteen steps going down from a hatch marked “coal.”

At the window she cuts her palm on the latch. He gives her his handkerchief, white linen with an H in green thread. She keeps it. Leaving, she gives him one true thing: “Bounds.” He asks for the rest. “Girls on Gin Lane don’t give the rest of it to a Harding.”

Felix Hawke calls at the tenement while Alice is out. The girl opens the door, frightened, and frost blooms across the frame under her hand, crrrkk. He leaves with a frozen palm and a question, and he writes it down.

Deep under the Works, something nine feet tall lifts its head for the first time in four hundred years. We see her face: blue ice, green fire in the eyes, hooks through her shoulders. Morgana.

“My daughter is here.”

Episode 3

“Lanterns”

42 Days to First Snow

Eleven years ago. A labour broker’s office: a ledger, a brass bell, the improvement of the labouring classes on the letterhead. Tobias Bounds signs without reading, watching a clerk count coin. He’s a vat-hand from the Harding Works, broad gone soft, with a tremor in his right hand and a fine green grit in the cracks of his knuckles that never washes out. His belt is cinched a notch too tight. Miriam, twelve, stands between the desk and her sister. Alice, ten, stands behind her.

“They’ll feed them. That’s more than I can do.”

“You said we were going to see the sea.”

He takes the coin and goes. Miriam’s hand is flat against the closing door; Alice’s hand is on Miriam’s sleeve.

Five years later: a labour-house dormitory, frost on the inside of the glass. Alice is asleep with bruised knuckles. A Vykra recruiter waits at the door. “One girl. The older one.” “She’ll follow me.” “Then she’ll be your collateral. That’s how this works.” “Everyone says that. Nobody says what it means.” “It means she is how we know you’ll come back.” Miriam signs. Her hand shakes. She crosses the room and kisses her sleeping sister’s forehead, the last free thing she does.

• • •

Grey light, a basin, a girl learning what a face is for. For a day and a half she is quiet, waiting. Then: “Alice.”

It’s Miriam’s voice. Alice’s hand goes still on the cup.

“I’m sorry. I — I have most of it. I think I have most of it. You’re my sister. We live together. I know that.” A pause that costs her. “I don’t know what we did yesterday.”

“Nothing. You slept.”

“Yes. Then I know that.”

She isn’t lying. She’s repeating the sentence she’s been told is true and hoping the rest arrives.

Eldgard gives Nathaniel the job he’s wanted his whole life: re-equip the Guard with his gauntlets and rigs for the Frost Killer hunt. In the corridor, out of earshot, he gives Nixon a different order. Find the frost girl. Bring her to the Works alive. Tell no one. The real son is arming the hunt. The spare is leading it. We flash to a charity school ten years back: Eldgard pricking boys’ fingers and touching the blood to slivers of gallowglass. Most stay dark. Thirteen-year-old Nixon’s glows green. Eldgard pats his head: “Still good.” It looks like kindness.

Four minutes’ warning of a Vykra welfare check. Alice moves through the room, kicking things under the cot. “She’ll ask what happened. You fell. You remember the water. You don’t remember anything else.”

“I remember the water.”

“Good. That’s the whole script.”

“What if I say the wrong—”

“Then they decommission you.”

The agent is kind, which is worse. The girl describes the cold of the water like a scientist and calls Alice the sister. The room goes quiet. “The sister.” Then she reaches for something she doesn’t understand, flat and bored and exactly Miriam: “Ask me again in a fortnight and I’ll be insufferable about it.” The agent laughs. “There she is. You had us worried, Bounds.”

The door closes. Alice listens to the footsteps go down two flights, tock, tock, tock, then sits down on the floor, which is not something she does.

“Was that right?”

“That was perfect.”

The first time she is truly Miriam, she’s impersonating her.

Alice visits Nathaniel at the Works “to return the handkerchief” and reads the sweep roster upside down while he shows off his boosters: one jump across the foundry and into the coal bin again, for an audience of one. She gets what she came for and leaves feeling sick at how easy it was, and sicker at how much she liked watching. She doesn’t return the handkerchief.

A tea stall under a broken awning, rain drumming the canvas. Nathaniel has worked out that the lamp-girl knows the Lanes, though not that she’s paid to watch him. “I can’t get into the rooms where Jamie’s death is being managed. You can’t get into the rooms where my brother’s friends drink.” She names a rate meant to end the conversation. He pays it without haggling, which insults her more than an argument would. “You’re a tourist in your own grief.” “My brother is dead and it’s the only thing I’ve been able to think about. I’ll take whatever gets me into that room.”

They write the terms on the back of a betting slip: she walks the Lanes for him, and he takes “Miss Bounds, factory-safety assessor” into society on his arm. She needs the rooms where a nobleman from the Hardings’ party drinks. She doesn’t say so. They’re both lying about why they want the door. She takes his hand to seal it. He’s the first person she has touched on purpose since the bridge.

Felix corners Nixon. There was a bullet hole in Jamie Harding’s coat. He slides a Newgate visitor’s slip across the desk: Jamie, every Sunday for two years, prisoner 1417. Both of Nixon’s secrets are walking toward each other.

Tamara has broken into her father Lord Lambrick’s cipher safe. She copies a ledger page: six months of payments to someone called A.F., ending the night Jamie died. On the stairs, Ajax is eating an apple in the dark. “Evening, miss.” He lets her pass.

• • •

The night of the sweep, the girl slips out of the tenement alone, drawn by a hundred lanterns singing at a Christmas crystal market. She walks barefoot through the slush between the stalls, among roasting chestnuts and wet wool and spiced wine, and reaches toward them, enchanted. Every crystal flares at once. A lookout spots it. Nixon’s leapers launch across the stalls, flash-bursts strobe the crowd, and she screams. Every crystal in the market shatters. Glass rains on the crowd. Four people die.

Nixon corners her alone in a bell tower. She reaches for Cole and freezes him from the hand up. Nixon can take the girl to Eldgard or carry Cole to a furnace before his heart stops. He takes Cole. She runs. Alice is on the other side of the city with a Harding.

Cole lives but loses two fingers. Eldgard finds Nixon at the hospital. “You had her and you let her go.” Then, softly: “I know what you did at the core, Captain. So do you.” The leash tightens.

Alice wakes to an empty cot and runs into the fog.

St Bride’s, past midnight. A barefoot girl knocks at the side door. A young priest opens it, tired from eleven years of night duty, his cassock shiny at the cuffs and a candle stub in his hand. “You’re bleeding, child. Come inside.”

“I’m a sinner. Please — convert me.”

He gives her tea, a blanket and gentle questions. And the building notices her. Every green lamp in the nave leans toward her. The organ pipes hum, mmmmmmm, a note nobody is playing. Somewhere far below, something that has slept four hundred years turns over.

“You have to go.”

“You said I could—”

“I’m sorry. I’m sorry. You have to go.”

Alice finds her on the steps holding a blanket she was given and then sent away with. “What did you do?”

“I asked to come in.” A beat. “They said no.”

“Good. Stay away from this place.”

She killed four people tonight, went looking for forgiveness, and doesn’t know yet what she is.

Episode 4

“The Hearing”

38 Days to First Snow

Miriam’s last day, before the bridge. She walks the factory streets hollow-eyed, carrying a weight that doesn’t show, past the vat-hands coming off shift with green grit in their collars. In the laundry room she sits on the cot, Alice’s cot, and writes: “Alice. I know you think you failed me. You didn’t. The world failed both of us. But I can’t stay here anymore. Not after what happened. Not after what I let him do…” She seals it, puts it under her pillow, and walks toward the bridge.

Present day. The tenement smells of onions and burnt sugar. She’s making their mother’s recipe, the one Miriam hated, and humming songs with the wrong words. Alice watches from the door.

“Remember when we snuck into the factory owner’s garden?”

“Of course. The roses were beautiful.”

A beat. “We never did that.”

“I… I must have imagined it.”

On the table lies a news-sheet: FROST KILLER — FOUR DEAD AT THE CRYSTAL MARKET. A dark-haired girl, barefoot. Alice reads it twice, folds it, and puts it in the stove. She watches it burn all the way down.

The Crown’s hearing on the Harding Charter is in three days. Josephine reads the witness list and stops at one name: Inspector Felix Hawke, testifying on the death of James Harding. A bullet hole in front of the Crown means an inquiry, and an inquiry goes down the core stair and finds what’s waiting there.

• • •

The morning after the forehead kiss, five years ago. Alice, fifteen, stands at the door of the Vykra training house. She packed nothing; she has nothing to pack. “The offer was for one.” “Then I’ll make you buy me too.” The recruiter, Grise, younger, her hair still dark, studies her for a long time. “You have wolf’s eyes, girl.” From the stair, Miriam: “Don’t. Don’t do this.” Alice walks in.

Miriam signed her in as collateral to keep her safe. Alice made herself a blade instead, and Miriam had to watch what her sister became.

Late. “Alice… do you ever feel like you’re not who you’re supposed to be?”

“What do you mean?”

“Sometimes I have dreams. And I’m not me. I’m something else. Something cold and alone.”

“You’re tired. You need sleep.”

The next night, by one candle. “You don’t sleep either.”

“I don’t trust sleep. Things happen when you’re not watching.”

“I have memories. But they feel… borrowed. Like I’m wearing someone else’s clothes.”

Something cracks behind Alice’s eyes. “Then we’ll figure out who you are. Together.”

The girl reaches for her hand. Alice lets her take it.

Nathaniel follows Alice into the street where she grew up: a court of soot-black brick with one pump, washing strung window to window, and children with bare feet in December. A woman who once shared bread with her spits near his shoe. “Got yourself a gentleman, Alice. Hope he pays better than the last one.” A man who knew their father calls her a collaborator. Nathaniel stands there and takes it, which she doesn’t expect. Then the neighbours mention that Miriam came through three weeks before she died, asking where their father had gone. She said she had news.

Alice hears news and takes it to mean Miriam was going back to the man who hurt them.

Nathaniel and Alice ambush Ajax at the coal docks. It’s a brutal fight: Ajax’s Black Gauntlet hits like a steam hammer, WHUMM, and Nathaniel recognises his own stolen prototype in it. They hook Ajax onto a crane over the river. Upside down, Ajax laughs. “Your brother paid me. Lambrick paid me too, but your brother paid double. I cut the outer grid. I never touched him.” Then: “Your father went down after. Ask the Guard who fired.”

Then he looks past Nathaniel, at Alice. “I know your face. Your sister’s, anyway. She’s meant to be in the river.”

Alice goes very still. “Pull him up.”

Nathaniel’s hand is on the lever. The man who helped kill his brother, or the one voice that can put Lambrick in the dock. Alice doesn’t tell him why she needs Ajax alive. Nathaniel lets go. Ajax drops into the Thames, SPLOOSH, and her one lead goes under with him.

Nixon, alone and in civilian clothes, visits prisoner 1417 in Newgate: an old man named Silas Marsh, in the dark, picking at a string he calls his harp. He pinches Nixon’s cheek through the bars: “Marian’s chin! Marian’s chin!” Then he sings: “Seven in the Orchard, ripe for the fall. One on the tree for the lantern’s call. Thirteen steps down where the cold water drips, count the apples, kiss the stones’ lips.” Nixon leaves with a nursery rhyme and a cold feeling he can’t name.

• • •

A tea room on the edge of the Narrows, with lace on the tables and a cracked cake stand. Josephine intercepts “Miriam” after a shift at the laundry. “My agents tell me Alice is distracted by her sister’s return. You will leave the city. I’ve arranged passage. Do not make me insist.”

The girl looks at her with an ancient, unblinking stillness. “I’m not leaving Alice.”

“I can have you removed.”

The temperature drops. Frost feathers across the teapot.

“You think you know all the secrets in your house, Lady Harding. But you missed one. The day the latch was left undone. The little green turtle in your drawing room.” Josephine freezes. “You were with the young solicitor. Your husband’s solicitor. On the rug. You told him you loved him, but you couldn’t leave.” A pause. “I won’t tell Alice. And I won’t tell your husband. Because I don’t care. But you will leave Alice and me alone.”

Josephine now knows exactly what lives in her watcher’s flat. She waits.

The hearing. Lambrick argues the Hardings are hiding something. During the recess a veiled woman in the back row crosses to the witness stand with a glass of water. “You look pale, Inspector.” Felix drinks.

He stands to testify. “The deaths at the market were caused by a girl who should be dead, and the Harding Works—” He coughs. Frost spreads across his lips. His eyes go white. He dies on the stand. The Frost Killer is blamed, and the vote swings toward the Hardings.

That night Nixon finds his own rifle in the discharge log: one round, 2 November. He tears the page out and burns it. From the bunk-room door, Cole watches. Later he copies the page from memory into his diary.

Josephine lifts her veil and peels off gloves with a Vykra knot stitched inside. She washes frost-dust from her fingertips, and the water skins over with ice. In the mirror, Byron is standing in the doorway.

Alice stands in the doorway of the tenement bedroom, over her sleeping sister, with a dagger at her side. For a long moment we don’t know.

“Alice…?”

She puts it away and closes the door.

Dawn. The dagger lies on the table. In voiceover: “If she’s a monster, she doesn’t know it. If she’s my sister, she’s broken. Either way — I need to know for certain.” The dagger goes into her belt. “I’ll find the truth. And then I’ll decide.”

Episode 5

“Lantern Night”

33 Days to First Snow

1489 again: the raid that followed the first episode’s prologue. Young Hargrave cousins with torches, and leading them the girl from the treeline, reaching for the iron chain with a hammer. Alistair cuts her down at the lock. Someone screams “Marian!” By dawn the empty gallows field is full: three thousand Hargraves on Harding timber. Their wives and children are walked down thirteen steps into the dark. This heist has been tried before.

Eldgard finally brings Nathaniel into “the work,” a cathedral of glass vats where engineers grow gallowglass from Tyburn iron. Nathaniel’s joy is real and painful to watch.

Byron gives Nathaniel his old Guard key on a black ribbon. Jamie borrowed it in October, had it copied, and left a note: “If I don’t come up, give it to Nat.” Byron guarded the core thirty years ago. “I heard her every night. I stopped hearing it when I stopped letting myself.” He hasn’t carried a green lamp since.

Klang. “This isn’t sickness. This is engineering. I’ve only felt this cold once, and it came from the Harding estate. You don’t need a doctor. You need an architect. Or an exorcist.”

Miriam’s body remembers the belt, and the mine smell their father brought home on his clothes. It’s the first time her fear isn’t her own.

• • •

On Lantern Night, London’s festival of light, four plans converge on the Works. Nathaniel’s crew, with Alice and Tamara, breaks in through a frozen outfall pipe. Lambrick’s men plan to cut the grid and embarrass the Hardings. Nixon has orders to let Nathaniel through and seal the core behind him.

They go down. And we see her whole for the first time. Morgana: nine feet of ice and green fire, iron hooks through her shoulders and hips, her blood running along channels into London. She’s singing the Weeping, the tune under every church hymn in London, stripped of its words.

She turns her vast head toward the Bounds girl on the gantry and breathes in, a long, slow, glacial hhhhhhh. “You smell of my daughter.”

“I don’t know your daughter.”

To Nathaniel, almost gently: “You came down these stairs once, little Harding. And your brother is not dead. He is cold.”

Nathaniel touches the chain and every lamp in London surges on his blood. Morgana screams.

Across London, in a tenement room on Gin Lane, a girl sitting on a cot screams back.

Every lamp in the city blazes white for one heartbeat and bursts. Street after street, glass rains on the festival crowds. People die against the railings on Westminster Bridge.

Eldgard steps out of the dark. “Now you know what your brother knew.” The Tithe: every fifty years, at first snow, a Harding walks down to the chain willingly, or it breaks. Jamie was raised for it. Jamie ran. “Now it’s you.”

The core door seals. Behind the glass is Nixon, following orders, his lips moving on the oath he swore on his first night as captain: Light the dark. Keep the flame. Over and over. Nathaniel sees his face through the glass.

Alice blows the door. She reaches for Nathaniel. He stays. “I need to hear the rest.” She leaves him there. On the way out through the Works office she takes a vat-hands’ roster, because it says Gin Lane on the spine.

Father and son, alone with the prisoner. “She said Jamie isn’t dead. She said he’s cold.” “She tells every Harding whatever will bring him to the lock. She told me my sister was still singing.” Then: “Thirty-three days. Help me find another way.”

Nathaniel says yes. He chooses his father.

Alice comes home through streets of broken glass that crunches under her boots, past people sitting on kerbs holding their own faces. Every window on the landing is blown in. Theirs is blown out.

“Were you hurt?”

“No.”

Alice looks at the window, the glass lying on the outside of the sill. Then she looks at her sister for a long moment. She doesn’t say anything, and she doesn’t ask. She files it with the others.

The next morning she writes her Vykra report on Nathaniel Harding. She leaves out the core, the chain and what his father said, and writes no contact of interest. It’s the first lie she has ever told the people who hold her sister. She stands at the drop a second too long.

Episode 6

“Coal”

28 Days to First Snow

Thirty years ago, a young Byron, Guard sergeant on night watch, catches a Vykra girl sent to kill young Eldgard. It’s Josephine, nineteen, and she’s caught because she stopped: behind the core gate a woman was weeping, and she listened a breath too long. He lets her go. A year later she marries the man she was sent to kill.

“I know what she did.”

“Who.”

“I know.”

Nobody buried Miriam. There was nothing to bury. At a river-edge chapel of the poor, with whitewashed walls, a tin cross and benches that smell of the tide, Alice buys a plain box and puts in what she has: a hairpin, a scrap of the letter, and the knife Miriam taught her to hold. Across the street Alice watches the door, and notes which priest goes in after her.

Episode 7

“The Girl Who Isn’t”

21 Days to First Snow

The White. Wind and nothing else, drifts that go on forever under a sky the colour of milk. A small thing of frost, abstract and wrong and beautiful, plays in the snow. Something vast watches over her from the storm, patient as weather.

On a roof, Nixon tells Nathaniel the truth. “I fired the shot. I didn’t know it was him.” Nathaniel hits him. KRAK. “Was Jamie alive when you left?” “Yes.” Fifteen years of friendship ends on a roof.

Alice is mending her coat, scorched at the gasworks, when her needle catches on paper in the lining of Miriam’s old jacket. A letter, never sent. It’s about the guilt and about wanting to say sorry. She reads it standing up and then she isn’t standing up any more.

She comes home and finds “Miriam” crying, for the first time ever. “I felt your pain. When you read it. I don’t understand how.”

Alice sits beside her. Their shoulders touch.

“We never talked about what happened to us. With him.”

“I remember being scared. I remember wanting to protect you.”

“You didn’t.”

“I know. I’m sorry.”

It isn’t Miriam apologising. It’s the Lost One saying the words Miriam couldn’t.

“I spent so long hating you for freezing,” Alice says. “But I froze too. When you jumped.”

They hold each other. The wound begins to heal, for the wrong reasons, with the wrong person.

• • •

The steam laundry where the girl takes shifts: rows of iron presses hissing, chff, chff, chff, sheets hanging in long white aisles, the air wet enough to drink. Ajax has come to collect the face he filed in the first episode. Lambrick wants the frost girl for his glass. Six men move among the presses.

Alice draws two knives. A throat is cut into a mangle and the linen goes red. Elbows, a headbutt, knnk, a cut to the arm. There’s blood on her teeth and she’s smiling. The fourth man gets her from behind with a blade at her throat.

Before any green, the girl does the old thing. She steps in front of Alice and takes the blow. It’s Miriam’s exact movement, on a body that isn’t hers. For one second her sister is back, and then she isn’t.

Then the eyes go green. The man flies twenty feet into the machinery, and the press doesn’t stop. The last two run.

Alice is shaking. “What the fuck was that?” She can’t stop. “Your eyes glowed green. You threw a man twenty feet. MIRIAM CAN’T DO THAT.”

“I remember jumping. I remember the water. I remember… not drowning. Something else happened.” The girl’s hands are open, pleading. “I feel like Miriam. I have her memories. But there are gaps. And something else is there. Something that was… trapped.”

And Alice puts it together, because she’s had the pieces since the night the window blew outward: the turtle, the glass case in Josephine’s drawing room, the thing Jamie broke out.

“You’re not my sister. You never were.”

“I have her memories. I felt her love for you. I felt her guilt. I tried to say what she couldn’t—”

“Don’t. Don’t you DARE pretend to be her.”

“I’m not pretending! I don’t know what I am! But I know — I know I don’t want to lose you.”

“Miriam is dead. Isn’t she?”

“I think so. I’m sorry.”

“I lost my sister weeks ago. Get out.”

She doesn’t yet understand that humans push people away when they’re terrified, so she obeys. She walks out into the dark. Alice slides down the laundry wall in the rain, alone by choice.

Nathaniel tracks her through three districts. He finds her on a roof in the rain with her legs over nothing.

“I’ve been protecting a monster.”

“You’ve been protecting someone who saved your life.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“Isn’t it?” He sits beside her. “You knew. You just couldn’t afford to believe it.”

She doesn’t argue, which is how he knows he’s right. He passes her a flask. Their shoulders touch. Before they stand, she says: “Your father signed the order that sent mine to the mines.”

“I know. I found the page under the others.”

The girl walks to the only place she knows: Blackwood Bridge, where Miriam went in. She cries. The tears freeze in the air, tink, tink, and the thin place on the bridge cracks. Every gallowglass lamp along the parapet turns toward her, and her mother’s voice comes out of the light.

“Hello, child. I’ve been looking for you.”

“I know you.”

“You forgot. Bodies make us forget.” The light leans closer. “They reject you. They will always reject you. Come home.”

“She told me to leave.”

“And you believed her? She is human. They break what they cannot control. You’ll stay with me. Forever. Safe.”

“…I don’t want to be safe. I want to be free.”

“Then I will take you by force.”

The lamplight wraps around her like rope. She screams, in rejection rather than fear. Green fire goes out of her and every lamp on the bridge bursts. “I don’t want to hurt you. But I will.”

From the dark glass, her mother answers. “Then I’ll call you when you cannot refuse.”

Wolves of the White pad out of the crack in the fog and bow to her.

Wolves pour through the streets of Whitechapel. Nixon’s Lanterns fight in strobe, every flash showing a wolf somewhere closer. At a music hall, a singer keeps a hundred people singing “Daisy Bell” to drown out the scratching at the doors. Nathaniel and Nixon arrive from different rooftops. No words. They fight back to back.

The girl walks into the street of wolves and speaks one word in a language of wind. They stop. She walks them back through the fog toward the bridge. A hundred people watch. By morning the papers will call her the Frost Witch.

• • •

Dawn. She’s asleep sitting up against the wall of the tenement stair, as far away as “get out” would let her go. Alice steps over her on the way to work. Neither of them speaks. On the third step, Alice leaves her coat on the landing.

That night Alice is patching her arm at the table.

“Are you afraid of me?”

“I’m afraid of needing you. Which is stupid, because you’re not even—”

“Not even Miriam. I know.”

“That’s not what I was going to say.”

“Yes it was.”

Alice ties off the thread with her teeth. “I mourned her. For weeks. And then you came back, and I thought — maybe I didn’t have to mourn anymore.”

“But you do.”

“I don’t know HOW.”

The girl reaches for her hand. Alice pulls away. “You can stay here. For now. But stay out of my way.”

Nathaniel comes up the stairs with a sack of coal. The girl lifts her head and sniffs the air. “You smell like the man who keeps my mother.”

At St Bride’s, Nathaniel breaks open his brother’s coffin. Kkrrrnk. Inside are three sacks of grey slag and a family Bible for weight. No body.

Tamara finds the Heartglass folio in the seized Harding files. One line: Vessel: White-born. She hands it to Alice. The cage Nathaniel built with his father was built for the girl on the stairs.

Alice reads it and hits Nathaniel harder than he hit Nixon. He didn’t know. It doesn’t matter.

Then Tamara arrives with a lunacy order, her father’s, for the day she opens her mouth about his books. “A wife’s nearest relative is her husband. The wedding is in seven days. I’d rather it were you than Bethlem.”

Alice: “Marry her.” Both of them mean it, and both hate that they do.

Tamara gives Nixon a folder. “It has your name on it.” Then she kisses him, a week before her wedding. Nixon doesn’t open the folder.

Episode 8

“The Wedding”

14 Days to First Snow

Sixteen years ago. Nathaniel, five, follows a lullaby down forbidden stairs and finds a huge, gentle woman of ice who asks his name and sings it back to him. His father snatches him up and swings a lantern before his eyes. “You had a dream. You never heard anything.” It’s the “nightmare” he’s had his whole life.

The day before the wedding. The glasshouse workshop on the Harding estate: bread, eggs, a window cracked to let the steam out, half-built gauntlets on every surface, winter sun coming green through old panes. Alice has stayed the night. She calls him “Lord Harding” in a flat voice and he pretends to be offended. They bicker over who stole whose coat. It’s the only undisturbed happy scene of the season, and it lasts about four minutes.

Byron arrives with food he says was surplus, his burned side stiff under a fresh collar, and pours the tea as if he’d seen nothing. At the stove, quietly, he tells her: “I’ve known for some time who you report to. He doesn’t. I’d ask that he hear it from you, and soon.”

“Today. I’ll tell him today.”

She means it. Within hours the chance is gone.

The tenement at dusk, one candle. A lead has come in through the Vykra, and Alice is dressing to go.

“Whatever happens tonight—”

“Don’t.”

“I need to say this.” The girl is sitting very straight on the cot, hands flat on her knees. “I’m not Miriam. I never was. But the love I feel for you — that’s real. That’s mine. Not borrowed. Not inherited from her memories. Mine.” She swallows. “Being your sister, even for a few weeks, was worth existing for. You made me want to be human. You made me human.”

“You’re coming back,” Alice says. “Because I’m not losing another sister. Not again. Not ever. I’ll be home before dawn.”

“Promise?”

“Promise.”

The lead is Josephine’s, and it sends Alice across the city to an empty house. On the way back she passes Harding House on Nathaniel’s last night unmarried, and goes up. A warm kitchen, a fire, his hand on the back of her neck. She stays too long. She doesn’t tell him.

• • •

Eight Vykra in black masks surround the tenement with orders to take the creature alive. Green light gathers in the girl’s hands.

Then Byron comes up the stair, burned down one side from the gasworks, an old man with young eyes. “Not this.” He says it to the masks, to the house, to Josephine, who isn’t there. “Lady Harding has gone too far.”

He looks at her. “Run. Find the boy. Tell him—”

He doesn’t finish.

She runs through the fog with the Vykra behind her, and feels her mother in every lamp she passes. She could run to Alice. She stops. “If she catches me running to them… she’ll know where they are.”

The light speaks. “You signalled me. Why?”

“Because if you were hunting me, you weren’t hunting them. The people I love.”

“You sacrificed yourself. For humans.”

“That’s what humans do.”

A long pause from the lamps. “It’s what I did. Once. For you.”

She lets the Vykra take her. On the tenement window, written in frost: DON’T FOLLOW ME.

Dawn, the morning of the wedding. Nathaniel comes looking for Byron and finds him on the tenement stair, still holding his blades. In the jacket is a letter: To be opened upon my death.

“Mother… you did this.”

“I didn’t know it was her.”

“But you knew it was someone.”

“Yes.”

“Was any of it real?”

She can’t answer. “I don’t know where it changed” is the honest answer, and it sounds like the worst one possible. He takes the letter and Byron’s blades. “I’m done being a Harding. I don’t know what I’m done being with you.”

“Then you know I love you.”

Minutes later: wine on the rug, a chair overturned, one black silk curtain torn from its tieback. Tamara kneels by her brother and takes the signet off his finger before she’s decided anything.

Lambrick arrives inside the hour with Ajax. “Who was here?”

“No one. I found him like this.”

An alley three streets away. Alice wakes on the cobbles, gasping, her head spinning as if she’s been drugged. Hhh — hhh — hhh. In her pockets: Edmund’s address, a Crimson Club matchbook, and a torn strip of black silk with a silver L at the edge, wine-dark, with glass grit in the fibres. There’s glittering dust on her cuff. One hour is gone.

“No. No, I’m not losing him.”

In the core, Lambrick and Vane watch the Heartglass close around the girl beside Morgana. She speaks in the language of the White. “I’ve come to take your place.”

Episode 9

“The Snow Confession”

First Snow — Dusk to Midnight

Twenty years ago. Eldgard teaches five-year-old Jamie to skate, both of them laughing. “One day the city will need you to be very brave.” “I’ll be brave, Papa.”

The Heartglass, with snow falling through the vents. The girl isn’t afraid. She’s listening. Under the Works, a wall of Hargrave dead turn over in their sleep. “Then I’ll talk to them anyway. Someone should.”

The glasshouse, snow coming through the broken panes and settling on the half-built gauntlets. Alice tells Nathaniel before he can ask.
